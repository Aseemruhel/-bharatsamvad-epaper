## BRICS Needs an AGI Forum

On September 12 and 13, 2026, the leaders of BRICS and its newer partners are sitting together in New Delhi for the 18th summit. The official theme is safe and diplomatic: Building for Resilience, Innovation, Cooperation and Sustainability. Artificial intelligence, digital infrastructure, quantum computing and semiconductors are all on the table. The summit will likely end with a declaration, a shared supercomputing proposal for researchers, and a deep-tech startup fund.

But underneath the diplomatic language sits a question nobody is asking out loud: who gets to decide what intelligence itself will look like, once machines start doing the thinking, deciding and creating that used to be done only by humans? This is not a small technical question. It is a civilizational one. And BRICS, almost by accident of history, is the only grouping on earth big enough, diverse enough and independent enough to attempt an answer that is not simply a copy of what the United States or China has already decided for everyone else.

## The Real Choice Delhi Is Facing

Strip away the press releases, and BRICS in Delhi faces one real choice. It can continue as it has: a respectful stakeholder that discusses AI governance, signs joint statements about fairness and inclusion, and asks to be consulted in bodies already dominated by Washington and Beijing. Indian officials have floated ambitions along these lines, including a proposed international body chaired by India to build shared risk assessments for AI, finance and climate.

Or, BRICS can treat this summit as the starting point of what deserves to be called a civilizational non-alignment project. A dedicated BRICS AGI Forum whose explicit purpose is not just to regulate AI safely, but to build advanced AI systems that carry Global South ways of thinking, teaching, creating and organising society, before Western-designed systems become so deeply embedded in daily life across these countries that reversing course is no longer realistically possible. These are not equally attractive options if you look honestly at how deep American AI capability has already travelled into BRICS countries, not through invasion, but through war, competition and everyday convenience.

## Three Pillars That Show the Problem

Russia is the BRICS country most openly at odds with the West. Its war in Ukraine has become one of the largest live experiments in military AI in modern history. Both sides use AI-assisted drones, automated targeting systems and data-fusion tools. Russia has pushed AI into machine-vision-guided loitering munitions and drone swarms. Yet Ukraine's war effort runs substantially on American AI tools, from satellite intelligence to commercial targeting software. So even in a war where Russia is fighting the American-led security order as directly as any country on earth, the AI ecosystem of the battlefield is saturated with US-origin technology on one side, forcing Russia to develop its own systems in reaction. Russia is not opting out of the American AI century. It is being shaped by it, even while fighting it. Military conflict does not create AI independence. It creates AI entanglement, just with more casualties.

China presents the opposite case. Chinese labs like DeepSeek have built genuinely impressive, low-cost AI models that rival American systems on many tasks. This has visibly worried Washington. But notice what this rivalry is actually about: it is a race to build the same kind of AI, chatbots, coding assistants and image generators optimised for the same benchmarks and the same use cases. China's challenge to America is a challenge of speed and cost, not yet a challenge to the underlying vision of what AI is for. China has not yet proposed a different paradigm. It is proving it can compete inside the American paradigm.

India's own record is the most instructive. In early 2026, India hosted the AI Impact Summit at Bharat Mandapam, the first major global AI summit held in the Global South, producing the New Delhi Declaration and new mechanisms including a Global AI Impact Commons. India explicitly emphasised AI for public health, agriculture, education and climate adaptation rather than the race to build the single smartest chatbot. This is a real act of agency. But India's frontier AI systems still overwhelmingly run on American chips, American cloud infrastructure and fine-tuned versions of American or Chinese base models. India has become skilled at applying AI to real social problems. It has not yet built an independent AI foundation of its own. Government policy talks about sovereignty, but the technical substrate underneath remains largely borrowed.

## The Deeper Question Nobody Is Really Asking

Most conversations about AI politics ask who will build the most powerful AI system first. This hides a bigger and stranger question: not who builds the most powerful AI, but whose way of thinking, whose idea of a good life or a successful business, gets baked permanently into the machines that increasingly do our thinking for us.

You have already seen a smaller, earlier version of this problem play out on social media. Algorithms built in California and Beijing decide what a teenager in Gurugram, a college student in Sao Paulo and a young professional in Lagos see and aspire to every single day. Indian Gen Z increasingly dresses, jokes and dreams in patterns shaped less by their grandparents' villages and more by trends manufactured in Los Angeles or Shenzhen. Advanced AI will not just recommend a video. It will write the essay, design the business plan, draft the school lesson and generate the film script. If these systems are trained mostly on Western-origin text and reflect Western assumptions about efficiency, rationality and individual success, they will not just entertain the world. They will quietly write its instruction manual.

## What BRICS Is Getting Wrong

BRICS is currently doing a bit of both without admitting it, pursuing shared computing infrastructure and AI startup funds while also talking about equitable access and inclusion, without ever explicitly weighing these two philosophies against each other in a single declaration. That ambiguity might be diplomatically convenient, but it guarantees that whichever path wins will do so by drift, not by decision, which is exactly the path dependence this argument has been warning about.

BRICS's real failure of imagination so far is that it has treated AI as a technical cooperation problem to be managed through committees and joint statements, when it is actually a civilizational choice about whose instincts get automated into the future. Refusing to make that choice consciously is itself a choice, and one that currently favours whichever power already has the most compute, the most data and the deepest head start. Delhi 2026 will be remembered either as the summit where BRICS quietly let that default continue, or as the one where it finally asked the harder question out loud.
