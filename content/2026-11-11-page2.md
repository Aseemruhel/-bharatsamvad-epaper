LEAD: An investigation by the Bangladeshi daily Prothom Alo, picked up and amplified by ANI News on 10 November 2026, has documented one of the clearest recent demonstrations of inauthentic anti-India content production targeting Chinese audiences. Approximately 150 Chinese nationals, operating in Dhaka, Bangladesh, filmed poor and working-class neighbourhoods — mainly in Tekpara and Uttara — and streamed the footage on the Chinese platforms WeChat and Douyin while telling their Chinese audiences they were watching New Delhi. On 1 November 2026, a Chinese vlogger identified as Mianmian streamed from near a canal in Uttara's Sector 12 and told viewers, in her own words: This is New Delhi. Their homes are extremely dirty. A second vlogger, identified as Wang Wei, filmed tea vendors in the Tekpara area and told his audience that the locals drink cow urine tea. A third, identified as Fangyou, told Prothom Alo directly that the streams were part of commercial promotion. Some of the Chinese participants claimed on camera that they were married to Indians and living permanently in New Delhi — claims that Prothom Alo describes as unfounded. Local Bangladeshi guides were paid 700 to 800 taka per day to help the foreign nationals navigate the neighbourhoods. Residents complained that the crews filmed without permission and that vloggers entered private homes and kitchens without consent. Uttara West Police Station has confirmed sighting the foreigners filming. The incident operationally captures what coordinated, inauthentic, anti-India content production looks like in practice — filmed in Dhaka, labelled as New Delhi, pushed to Chinese-platform audiences on a scale that single operators cannot manufacture alone.

## What Prothom Alo actually documented

The Prothom Alo investigation establishes a specific operational pattern that is worth reading in full. The approximately 150 Chinese nationals who have been operating in Dhaka selected poor and working-class neighbourhoods — Tekpara and Uttara being the two principal filming locations identified by name — and conducted live streams in which the geographic label applied to the footage was consistently wrong. The footage was of Dhaka. The label was New Delhi. The audience watching on WeChat and Douyin — the two principal Chinese-market social-media platforms — was told, in Chinese, that it was watching life in the Indian capital.

The individual streams that Prothom Alo documented by name carry the operational signature of coordinated production rather than organic travel vlogging. Mianmian, who told Prothom Alo she was from Sichuan, streamed near a canal in Uttara's Sector 12 on 1 November 2026 and told her viewers: This is New Delhi. Their homes are extremely dirty. The sentence is not a travel observation. It is a scripted framing — a geographic misattribution paired with a derogatory sanitary judgement — deployed from the opening seconds of the stream. Wang Wei's framing is similar: filming Bangladeshi tea vendors in Tekpara, he told his audience that locals drink cow urine tea. The characterisation is both false as a description of the Dhaka tea culture he was filming and is lifted from one of the oldest anti-Indian stereotypes in East Asian online discourse.

Fangyou's admission to Prothom Alo that the streams were part of commercial promotion is operationally important. The admission frames the activity as a commercial content operation — not an incidental tourist encounter, not an organic cultural exchange — within which a specific geographic misattribution is being deployed as the product.

## Why this is operationally significant

The three dimensions of the Prothom Alo investigation that deserve particular attention are: the scale, the geography, and the platform.

The scale — approximately 150 Chinese nationals operating a coordinated content pattern in Dhaka — is not what organic travel vlogging looks like. A tourist filming their own observations is a single person with a phone. A commercial content operation involving 150 participants producing streams of poor neighbourhoods labelled as a different country's capital, routed through a single pair of platforms, paying local guides on a standardised daily rate, is operationally an organised content production — coordinated, commissioned, and deployed.

The geography — filmed in Dhaka, labelled as New Delhi — is the specific form of inauthenticity that defines the operation. The content is not fabricated in the sense of being computer-generated or staged in a studio. The streets, the canal, the tea vendors, the homes, and the residents are all real. The misattribution is the manufactured element. The audience is told they are seeing India when they are seeing Bangladesh. Every negative association the audience forms from the footage — the dirt, the poverty, the alleged dietary habits — is loaded onto India through an operationally dishonest geographic label.

The platform — WeChat and Douyin — is the Chinese-market closed-information environment. Both platforms operate under Chinese state content governance. Both are the primary social-media platforms for the Chinese-speaking audience inside the People's Republic of China. Content produced and distributed on these platforms is not reaching international audiences outside the Chinese information space. The target audience for the Dhaka-as-New-Delhi operation is the Chinese domestic audience. The operational purpose is to shape Chinese domestic perception of India — not to influence Indian, Bangladeshi, or Western viewers.

## The pattern this fits

The Dhaka-as-New-Delhi operation is not an isolated episode. It fits a pattern of Chinese-platform content production in which negative material filmed in third countries is passed off as being filmed in India, targeting Chinese domestic audiences who have limited independent means of verification. The pattern has been documented repeatedly over the past several years across multiple Chinese platforms, typically featuring Chinese-language commentary, Chinese-platform distribution, and geographic misattribution of the kind Prothom Alo has now documented in Dhaka.

The operational signature is consistent. A Chinese-language audience is told it is seeing India. The footage is of a lower-income neighbourhood in a third country — or, in some earlier documented cases, in rural China itself. A derogatory framing is scripted into the opening seconds. The content reaches an audience that has no straightforward mechanism to verify the geographic claim. The operation runs at low cost and at scale. And the aggregate effect on Chinese domestic perception of India — across millions of viewers watching thousands of streams across hundreds of operators — is a progressively distorted picture that Indian diplomatic outreach, Indian commercial investment, and Indian cultural communication have no straightforward way to correct.

## Bangladesh's response — and what Dhaka needs to do

The Bangladeshi response to the Prothom Alo investigation has two dimensions. The first is the Uttara West Police Station's confirmation that it has sighted the foreigners filming. The second is the administrative statement that the reasons for the misrepresentation are under administrative review. Both are preliminary responses to what Prothom Alo has documented. Neither yet constitutes the kind of enforcement action — visa review, deportation, or legal action against commercial content misrepresentation — that the scale of the operation would appear to warrant.

For Bangladesh, the operational question is why approximately 150 Chinese nationals have been able to operate a coordinated content production in Dhaka at a scale that involves filming poor neighbourhoods without permission, entering private homes and kitchens without consent, and systematically misattributing the location to a third country, without any Bangladeshi regulatory response at an earlier stage. Prothom Alo's investigation has done what a free press is supposed to do — document the pattern and bring it into public view. The follow-on question is whether Bangladeshi authorities will now act on what their own newspaper has documented.

## What it signals for India

For India, the Dhaka-as-New-Delhi operation operationally documents one specific kind of digital perception-management risk — the inauthentic third-country content operation targeting a closed information environment. India's own public communication apparatus — the Ministry of External Affairs, Indian missions abroad, and Indian platform engagement with Chinese audiences — has very limited capacity to reach the WeChat and Douyin audience directly. The closed-information environment in which the operation is distributed means that a corrective Indian message does not reach the Chinese domestic viewer. The audience that watches Mianmian tell them Dhaka is New Delhi has no routine mechanism to encounter a correction from anyone who knows what New Delhi actually looks like.

The implications extend beyond the specific operation. India's broader strategic communication challenge toward Chinese audiences — the India Development Partnership Administration's outreach, the Ministry of External Affairs' international campaigns, Indian corporate communications, and the broader cultural diplomacy anchored by yoga, cinema, cuisine, and Indian technology brand presence — all face the same closed-platform access challenge. Operations like the one Prothom Alo has documented operationally exploit that asymmetric platform access.

## What it signals for the wider pattern

For the wider international documentation of Chinese-platform content operations, the Dhaka-as-New-Delhi incident is operationally useful as a near-perfectly documented case. Prothom Alo's investigation names participants, locations, dates, platforms, payment rates, and specific scripted quotations. The documentation is complete enough to serve as a reference case for how coordinated inauthentic content production actually functions when it is caught in the act.

The incident also captures the operational cost advantage of this kind of content production. The combined operational cost of filming in Dhaka — local guide rates of 700 to 800 taka per day, no infrastructure investment, no studio production, no digital fabrication — runs into a few thousand taka per stream. The audience reach across WeChat and Douyin runs into thousands of viewers per stream, and across approximately 150 operators running multiple streams each, into audience numbers that operationally shape Chinese domestic perception of India at a cost that would be trivial for any commissioning entity.
