LEAD: The composition of India's power generation architecture, as it stands on the morning of 3 September 2026, tells one of the most consequential structural stories of the country's post-Independence trajectory. Coal — the fuel that anchored India's electricity system for the entire twentieth century — now accounts for 40.8 per cent of the 552 GW total installed capacity. Solar, which stood at just 2.8 GW in 2014, has climbed to 29.5 per cent. Wind contributes 10.5 per cent. Hydropower — combining large hydro and pumped storage — accounts for 9.5 per cent. Nuclear stands at 1.6 per cent. Gas, lignite, biomass, small hydro, waste-to-energy and diesel together make up the remainder. Non-fossil sources — solar, wind, hydro, nuclear, biomass and small hydro combined — now account for over 54 per cent of installed capacity, the historic crossover Bharat Samvad's 19 August Page 1 coverage documented in detail.

This piece captures what the 19 August coverage could not: the internal composition of that 54 per cent share, and the forward-looking projection that positions India's power mix at a categorical inflection point over the coming decade. The Central Electricity Authority's (CEA) National Generation Adequacy Plan for 2026-27 to 2035-36, released earlier this year, projects the composition itself will invert by the end of FY2035-36. Solar capacity is projected to expand from 141 GW at the start of FY2025-26 to 509 GW by end-FY2035-36 — becoming India's single largest source of installed generation capacity, ahead of coal (projected 315 GW). Wind is projected to reach 155 GW. Large hydro to 78 GW. Nuclear to 22 GW under the 100 GW-by-2047 trajectory. Energy storage — the operational partner that renewable generation requires to deliver reliable dispatchable power — is projected to reach 174 GW / 888 GWh, comprising 80 GW / 321 GWh of battery energy storage systems (BESS) and 94 GW / 567 GWh of pumped storage plants (PSPs).

## The composition, unpacked

India's 552 GW installed base is not a single quantity but a composition of substantially different generation technologies, each with distinct operational characteristics, dispatch profiles, and forward trajectories. Understanding the composition matters because the sources' operational behaviours differ categorically.

Coal (40.8 per cent, ~225 GW). Coal-fired thermal power remains India's baseload workhorse. Its 40.8 per cent share of installed capacity translates to a substantially higher share of actual generation — approximately 70-75 per cent — because coal plants run continuously at high capacity factors while renewable generation is intermittent. This gap between installed capacity share and actual generation share is one of the important honest caveats to India's clean-energy transition arithmetic. It will progressively close as renewable capacity expands and energy storage matures, but for now coal remains India's operational baseload backbone.

Solar (29.5 per cent, 164.59 GW). Solar has become India's single largest renewable technology, and its 29.5 per cent share of installed capacity — up from under 1 per cent in 2014 — represents one of the most consequential single-technology expansions in India's post-Independence infrastructure history. Solar's capacity factor (approximately 18-22 per cent depending on location) means its actual generation contribution is proportionally smaller than its installed share, but the capacity build-out itself continues to accelerate. India added 34.95 GW of solar in FY2025-26 — the highest single-year addition in Indian history — and became the world's second-largest solar growth market in 2025, adding 37 GW compared with 34 GW added by the United States.

Wind (10.5 per cent, 58.14 GW). Wind has expanded from 21 GW in 2014 to 58.14 GW in 2026 — a 2.8x expansion, more measured than solar's 59x trajectory but nonetheless one of the most consequential renewable segments. Wind's capacity factor (typically 25-30 per cent in high-quality Indian wind zones) is higher than solar's, giving it proportionally larger generation contribution per gigawatt of installed capacity.

Hydro (9.5 per cent, ~57 GW). Combined large hydro and pumped storage plant capacity provides one of the important dispatchable renewable segments — hydro can be turned on and off in minutes to complement intermittent solar and wind, which is why 94 GW of pumped storage plants is projected in the FY2035-36 mix. Small hydro (under 25 MW capacity) counts within the wider renewable category.

Nuclear (1.6 per cent, 8.78 GW). Nuclear provides low-carbon baseload power at high capacity factor (India's nuclear fleet operated at 83 per cent load factor in FY2024-25). The 1.6 per cent share is small in installed capacity terms but the 2.9 per cent actual generation share reflects nuclear's operational reliability. Under the Nuclear Energy Mission announced in the Union Budget 2025-26 and documented in Bharat Samvad's 23 July Page 2 coverage, nuclear capacity is projected to expand progressively toward the 100 GW-by-2047 target.

Others (~8 per cent). Gas (~3.8 per cent), lignite (~1.2 per cent), biomass (~2 per cent), small hydro (~1 per cent), waste-to-energy (~0.2 per cent), and diesel (~0.1 per cent) together make up the remainder. Gas serves as a substantially cleaner fossil dispatchable source and progressively strategic import-substitution segment (as documented in Bharat Samvad's 20 August Page 2 coverage of the Nagaland National Gas Grid connection).

## The FY2025-26 solar record — 34.95 GW in a single year

FY2025-26 has recorded the highest annual solar capacity addition in India's history — 34.95 GW, compared with previous best-year additions of approximately 15-17 GW during FY2022-23 and FY2023-24. The doubling of annual addition rates reflects the operational maturity of the domestic solar manufacturing ecosystem, the substantive completion of the Approved List of Models and Manufacturers (ALMM) architecture that Bharat Samvad's 12 August Page 1 coverage examined in detail, and the accelerating deployment of the various policy instruments the Government has progressively built.

The 34.95 GW figure has three components that are worth understanding separately. Utility-scale solar projects — the large ground-mounted solar parks that anchor the majority of India's solar capacity — contributed the largest share. Rooftop solar deployment under the PM Surya Ghar Muft Bijli Yojana progressively contributed a substantive share, with 51.58 lakh households benefiting from rooftop solar installations by 12 August 2026, out of the scheme's 1 crore household target and ₹75,021 crore outlay. And hybrid solar-plus-storage projects — a substantively growing segment as energy storage economics improve — contributed the remainder.

The 37 GW total solar addition India recorded in calendar year 2025, positioning the country as the world's second-largest solar growth market ahead of the United States (34 GW), reflects the same trajectory as measured against calendar rather than fiscal year boundaries. China remains the world's largest solar growth market by a substantial margin, but the India-US crossover in 2025 represents one of the more consequential single moments in the global solar deployment ranking.

## The 2035-36 projection — solar as India's single largest source

The Central Electricity Authority's National Generation Adequacy Plan for 2026-27 to 2035-36 projects one of the most consequential structural transformations in India's power sector history. By end-FY2035-36 — approximately ten years from now — solar is projected to become India's single largest source of installed power generation capacity.

The specific projections are precise. Solar: 509 GW installed capacity, 45 per cent share of total. Coal: 315 GW installed capacity, 28 per cent share. Wind: 155 GW. Large hydro: 78 GW. Small hydro: 6 GW. Nuclear: 22 GW (with the Nuclear Energy Mission trajectory extending further toward 100 GW by 2047). Biomass: 16 GW. Gas: 20 GW.

The CEA's projection is anchored in what it describes as "complete visibility" into capacity additions through the projected year. This visibility includes 155 GW of solar, wind, hybrid and other renewable capacity currently under construction; 48 GW of renewable capacity under tendering; and 134 GW planned to be added under the Green Energy Corridor Phase III scheme. Additional capacity is progressively being planned and implemented at the state level. The forward trajectory is not aspirational — it is the sum of visible pipeline projects.

Coal, per the CEA's own assessment, will remain the operational backbone of India's electricity generation during the transition period, providing reliable baseload power that renewables cannot fully replace until energy storage matures at scale. The 28 per cent installed capacity share projected for FY2035-36 will translate to a substantially higher actual generation share due to coal's high capacity factor. But the direction is unmistakable: from 40.8 per cent share today toward 28 per cent by FY2035-36. Coal is not going away — it is progressively being displaced from marginal capacity additions in favour of cleaner alternatives.

## Energy storage — the operational partner renewable generation requires

The 174 GW / 888 GWh of energy storage capacity projected for FY2035-36 is the single dimension of the CEA's plan that receives least public attention but arguably matters most for actual operational delivery of clean-energy transition. Energy storage is the operational partner that intermittent renewable generation requires to deliver reliable dispatchable power to consumers.

The projected 174 GW comprises two categories. Battery energy storage systems (BESS): 80 GW / 321 GWh, primarily built around lithium-ion technology but progressively expanding to include sodium-ion, iron-air, and other emerging chemistries. Pumped storage plants (PSPs): 94 GW / 567 GWh, using the well-established technology of pumping water uphill during periods of surplus renewable generation and releasing it downhill through hydro turbines during periods of high demand.

The GWh figures — the total energy storage capacity, distinct from the GW power ratings — indicate how many hours of stored energy each system can deliver. 321 GWh across 80 GW of BESS implies approximately 4 hours of storage at full power rating. 567 GWh across 94 GW of PSPs implies approximately 6 hours. Together, this combined storage architecture provides India's power system with the operational flexibility to progressively increase renewable generation share while maintaining grid reliability.

## What it signifies

For India's power consumers, the projected mix means progressively cleaner electricity at progressively stable or declining tariffs. Solar tariffs have fallen from approximately ₹8 per unit in 2014 to around ₹2.50-3 per unit today at utility-scale bid levels. As storage economics improve and grid infrastructure matures, the combined solar-plus-storage tariff will progressively become competitive with coal-fired thermal power on both a marginal and average basis.

For India's climate commitments, the projected mix positions the country to meet or exceed every current international commitment. The 500 GW COP26 target for 2030 has been 60 per cent achieved four years early. The Paris Agreement NDC target of 50 per cent non-fossil installed share was crossed in June 2025, five years early. The 2070 Net Zero target requires progressive strengthening across every dimension of the Indian economy, but the power-sector trajectory captured in the CEA's plan progressively delivers the electricity-generation dimension.

For India's Aatmanirbhar Bharat framework, the projected mix expansion is progressively anchored in domestic manufacturing capacity that the ALMM framework, PLI scheme, and wider industrial policy have built. Every gigawatt of new solar capacity deployed in India progressively uses Indian-manufactured modules; every gigawatt of new wind capacity uses Indian-designed turbines; and progressively every gigawatt of energy storage will use Indian-manufactured battery cells as the Advanced Chemistry Cell (ACC) manufacturing PLI scheme progressively delivers capacity.

For India's global standing, the 3rd-place ranking in renewable energy installed capacity — behind only China and the United States — will progressively strengthen through the projected trajectory. India has demonstrated, on the operational record, that it can deliver ambitious clean-energy commitments at pace and scale that few other developing economies can match.

For now, on the morning of 3 September 2026, what stands on the operational record is this: India's power mix stands at 40.8 per cent coal, 29.5 per cent solar, 10.5 per cent wind, 9.5 per cent hydro, 1.6 per cent nuclear, and the balance across gas, biomass, small hydro, waste-to-energy, lignite and diesel; non-fossil sources exceed 54 per cent of installed capacity; FY2025-26 recorded the highest annual solar addition in Indian history at 34.95 GW; the Central Electricity Authority's National Generation Adequacy Plan projects solar becoming India's largest single source by FY2035-36 at 509 GW; energy storage of 174 GW / 888 GWh will progressively provide the operational partner that renewable generation requires; and the trajectory of India's power sector transformation continues to accelerate rather than plateau. From 40.8 per cent coal today to 28 per cent by FY2035-36. From 29.5 per cent solar today to 45 per cent by the same horizon. The composition itself is inverting — and the operational visibility into the pipeline confirms that the trajectory is not aspiration but delivery.
