LEAD: Some images do not merely record a moment; they turn a page of history. On the evening of Thursday, 7 August 2026, at Pandit Deendayal Upadhyay Auditorium in Raipur, when former women Maoists walked the ramp at the Swadeshi Fashion Show organised on the occasion of National Handloom Day, the past and the present walked together. Women whose lives once passed among the forests of Bastar, Dantewada, Sukma and Bijapur — amid weapons, insurgent operations and the fear that shaped every waking hour — stepped onto the ramp with the same self-possession one associates with professional models. Amid the sheen of Kosa silk, the quiet dignity of traditional tribal weaves, and the vivid palette of Chhattisgarhi handloom garments, their presence delivered a message that no security bulletin can record: rehabilitation is not the surrender of weapons alone; it is the beginning of a new life.

The event was not merely a platform to promote fashion and handloom. It was the story of those who have left the path of violence and taken a step toward the social mainstream. It was the story of an entire rehabilitation architecture that the Chhattisgarh government, central agencies and local communities have progressively built over recent years. And it was also the story of the weavers, artisans and traditional craftspeople whose skill gave these women's new lives a Swadeshi identity.

## From the forests to the ramp — a symbolic journey

The presence of these women on the ramp was symbolic at multiple levels. Hands that once held weapons today honoured the intricate weave of Kosa silk. Women who once passed the cold nights of the forest were, today, standing in a well-lit auditorium amid the applause of the audience. Women whose lives were once nameless, faceless and underground, today stood on the stage with a new identity.

This transformation did not come suddenly. Behind it stands a decade of security strategy, surrender policy, rehabilitation packages, skill-development programmes, and the long, gradual work of social acceptance. Against the scale of surrenders that Chhattisgarh has witnessed in recent years, this event carries particular significance. In January 2026, following the surrender of nine Maoists in Dhamtari district, the Raipur Police Range was declared entirely free of Naxal activity. Among the larger names, the surrender in March 2026 of the senior Bastar Maoist leader Papa Rao — along with eighteen of his associates — was covered in detail by Bharat Samvad in its 14 July edition. Women have featured prominently among those surrendering.

Officials note that those who surrendered cited disillusionment with Maoist ideology, the harsh realities of forest life, and the progressively improving alternatives available under rehabilitation schemes as their principal reasons. It is also true that alongside sustained security pressure, the SAMADHAN doctrine, the Puna Margem initiative, financial packages linked to surrender, and the strategy of reconnecting former cadres to local communities, have all played central roles in enabling this shift.

## Kosa silk — Chhattisgarh's cultural signature

The garments presented at Raipur featured a distinctive Kosa silk presence. Kosa — also known as Tussar silk — is among Chhattisgarh's most prestigious handloom products. Weavers in Bastar, Champa, Korba and Sarguja regions have practised its spinning and weaving across generations. Kosa silk is produced from the cocoons of silkworms that thrive on Sal trees — an entirely natural, sustainable, environmentally-benign process that has endured for centuries.

Events of this kind also seek to bring local weavers, artisans and traditional crafts into contact with new markets and new generations. In Chhattisgarh, the handloom sector is not merely a part of cultural heritage — it is the livelihood of thousands of families. The presence of former women Maoists on the ramp in this very traditional attire represented the confluence of two distinct journeys — from violence to the mainstream on one side, and from traditional craft to contemporary markets on the other.

## National Handloom Day — from 1905 to 2026

The date of National Handloom Day was not chosen at random. On 7 August 1905, at the Calcutta Town Hall, the Swadeshi Movement was formally launched. It was the mass movement that arose in protest against the Partition of Bengal, and it gave India's freedom struggle a new direction. The Swadeshi Movement conveyed for the first time that the boycott of foreign textiles and the use of Swadeshi products would not only awaken national self-respect, but would also secure the economic independence of local weavers, artisans and craftspeople.

Honouring this historical background, on 7 August 2015 Prime Minister Narendra Modi formally launched National Handloom Day from Chennai. Its purpose was to honour handloom weavers and carry the Indian handloom tradition to new generations. Alongside it, the India Handloom Brand — a distinctive certification mark — was launched, so that the quality and authenticity of Indian handloom products could be identified in global markets.

Over the past decade, thousands of programmes tied to handlooms have been organised across the country on this day. Weavers' fairs, handloom exhibitions, fashion shows, artisan-honouring ceremonies and educational programmes — through all these, a broad public awareness has been progressively built. Raipur's event this year was part of that tradition, but its distinctive feature was that the women walking the ramp in Swadeshi attire were themselves a living picture of India's social mainstream reception.

## Rehabilitation's success — security and social alike

Chhattisgarh has long been affected by left-wing extremism. But over the past decade, alongside security operations, sustained emphasis has been placed on surrender and rehabilitation policy. The results are visible in the numbers and on the ground.

The figures speak clearly. Since 2014, over 10,000 Maoists have surrendered. In 2025 alone, the number stood at 2,300, and in the first quarter of 2026, over 630. Districts formally classified as LWE-affected in 2014 numbered 126; by 2026, the figure has fallen to just 8. Raipur, Durg and Mahasamund Police Ranges have been declared Naxal-free. This is an unprecedented transformation in India's internal security history.

But on the ground, this transformation is not confined to security metrics alone. Its true measure lies in how well former Maoists are returning to dignified civilian life. The Government's SAMADHAN doctrine — Smart leadership, Aggressive strategy, Motivation and training, Actionable intelligence, Dashboard-based KPIs, Harnessing technology, Action plan for each theatre, No access to financing — dedicates its fifth pillar (Motivation and training) specifically to rehabilitation. Puna Margem — the special initiative launched at Bastar — focuses on providing skill development, financial packages, housing, healthcare, and educational opportunities for the families of former Maoists.

The presence of former women Maoists at the Raipur fashion show was a living testament to the success of this entire policy architecture. When a former Maoist appears on a social platform with dignity, identity and economic independence, it becomes a clear message to other former cadres that the path to the mainstream is open, safe and dignified.

## The particular challenges of women's rehabilitation

The rehabilitation of women involves particular challenges that differ from those faced by men. After spending years in the forests, when a woman returns to the social mainstream, she must navigate not just livelihood but also social acceptance, family responsibilities, and the complex process of building a new identity.

This is why skill-development programmes are especially important for women — programmes that give them economic self-reliance alongside social dignity. Handicrafts, tailoring and embroidery, weaving, local food processing and marketing of local products — these are options tied to traditional knowledge that provide steady income alongside self-reliance.

The handloom sector is particularly suited to this purpose because it links local traditions to employment opportunities. In Chhattisgarh, Kosa silk, Bastar's traditional weaves, and tribal textile crafts are not only the state's identity but also important livelihood sources. Over recent years, the government has launched multiple programmes to raise weavers' incomes, improve market access, and blend modern design with traditional craft.

## One ramp, many messages

The distinctive feature of Raipur's event lay in the fact that fashion here was not merely a medium of display — it was the vehicle for a comprehensive social, cultural and economic message.

 First message — rehabilitation is possible.  For those who once walked the path of violence, if they wish to leave weapons behind and return to a dignified life, the system is ready for them.

 Second message — the power of Swadeshi.  India's traditional weaving, local crafts and indigenous products are not only cultural heritage — they are potent instruments of economic empowerment.

 Third message — the role of women.  In the rehabilitation process, women's role is not only as beneficiaries but also as agents of change. When a former woman Maoist walks the ramp, she becomes a living inspiration for thousands of women like her.

 Fourth message — Chhattisgarh's new picture.  The state that was once known for Maoism is now emerging as a picture of rehabilitation, development, Swadeshi craft and Aatmanirbhar Bharat.

## The road ahead

The rehabilitation process in Chhattisgarh is not yet complete. In the remote regions of Bastar, Dantewada, Sukma and Bijapur, challenges of development, employment and social mainstreaming persist. Roads, schools, health centres, banking facilities, digital connectivity — these are the basic structures without which no rehabilitation programme can be fulfilled.

Both the central and state governments have invested substantially in these regions over recent years. Thousands of kilometres of roads are being built in the Bastar division, dozens of new schools, health centres, and mobile towers have been installed. This entire process will accelerate further in the coming years. Raipur's event demonstrated that when security, rehabilitation, development, and cultural identity come together, the results can be deeply inspiring.

From weapons to handlooms — this journey is not merely that of a few women; it is that of Chhattisgarh, of India, and of all those who wish to move from violence to skill, from struggle to prosperity, from the forests to the mainstream. The applause at Pandit Deendayal Upadhyay Auditorium was not only for those women — it was for the entire process that made India worthy of this day.
