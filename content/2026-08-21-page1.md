LEAD: One of the most consequential single days in the seven-decade India-Japan defence relationship took place on Thursday, 20 August 2026, in New Delhi. Defence Minister Rajnath Singh and his Japanese counterpart Shinjiro Koizumi — on his maiden two-day official visit to India — signed a landmark Memorandum of Arrangement on Maritime Security Cooperation that formalises the framework for expanded operational cooperation between the Indian Navy and the Japan Maritime Self-Defense Force (JMSDF). The agreement spans five substantive dimensions: joint naval ship design and manufacturing, defence technology transfers, enhanced Maritime Domain Awareness (MDA) information sharing, reciprocal ship repair and logistics support, and fast-tracking of the UNICORN integrated communications antenna project — India-Japan's first-ever defence equipment co-development programme. Alongside the maritime pact, DRDO and Japan's Acquisition, Technology and Logistics Agency (ATLA) will deepen cooperation on advanced and emerging defence technologies, and both countries will convene a new Defence Industry Forum to accelerate collaboration between their defence industries.

The signing comes six weeks after the 16th India-Japan Annual Summit at which Prime Minister Narendra Modi and Japanese Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi elevated the UNICORN project to full co-development status, describing it as opening "a new chapter in our defence technology partnership". The 20 August Ministerial Meeting operationalises that leadership-level commitment into the working-level architecture that will actually deliver the ships, systems, and shared operational capabilities the two countries have been progressively building toward. Modi received Koizumi at the Prime Minister's residence and, in a post on X, characterised the visit as advancing the Special Strategic and Global Partnership that remains "an important anchor for peace, stability and prosperity in the Indo-Pacific and beyond".

## What the pact actually covers

The Memorandum of Arrangement on Maritime Security Cooperation is not one instrument but the umbrella under which multiple operational tracks now proceed in parallel.

Joint naval ship design and manufacturing is the most substantial single dimension. Japan brings advanced warship-design expertise including its Mogami-class stealth frigate architecture. India brings its expanding shipbuilding capacity across Cochin Shipyard, Mazagon Dock, GRSE, Hindustan Shipyard, L&T Shipbuilding and the wider network of public and private yards documented in Bharat Samvad's 19 August Page 2 coverage. The pact provides the framework for joint design work and shared production runs, potentially transforming India from a domestic shipbuilder into a co-producer of Japanese-designed platforms for the Indian Navy and third-country customers.

Defence technology transfers cover the wider category of Japanese defence systems that will now progressively flow to India under Tokyo's April 2024 revised Three Principles on Transfer of Defence Equipment and Technology. Areas of Indian interest include seaplanes (ShinMaywa US-2 amphibious aircraft), conventional submarines (Soryu-class variant technology), marine engines, aero-engines, and military-grade semiconductors for avionics. Each of these categories has been under Indo-Japanese discussion for years but had been substantially constrained by Japan's pre-2024 export regulatory architecture.

Maritime Domain Awareness (MDA) information sharing formalises real-time exchange of maritime intelligence between the Indian Navy and JMSDF. This substantially extends the two navies' combined surveillance reach across the Indian Ocean and Western Pacific — the two theatres where Chinese People's Liberation Army Navy (PLAN) presence has been progressively expanding since the mid-2010s. With Indian assets primarily in the Indian Ocean Region and Japanese assets primarily in the Western Pacific, MDA information sharing creates a substantially wider composite coverage than either country can produce alone.

Reciprocal ship repair and logistics support opens Indian shipyards to Japanese naval vessels for maintenance, refit and repair, and opens Japanese naval facilities to Indian vessels on operational deployment. This substantially reduces the operational-cost overhead of extended deployments and provides both navies with substantially expanded logistics reach. It also substantially strengthens Indian shipyards' ability to build the specialised expertise required to service modern Japanese warships — a capability that will progressively be marketable to third-country navies.

Fast-tracking UNICORN operationalises the co-development programme that both leaderships have publicly committed to. Bharat Electronics Limited (BEL) manufactures the system in India under Japanese design and core technology inputs from NEC Corporation, Sampa Kogyo K.K., and Yokohama Rubber Co. Ltd. The system integrates multiple communication, surveillance and electronic warfare antennas into a single stealth-shaped mast, substantially reducing the radar cross-section of a warship's superstructure. Currently deployed on Japan's Mogami-class frigates, UNICORN will progressively equip Indian Navy surface combatants.

## The UNICORN co-development programme

UNICORN — the Unified Complex Radio Antenna, also known as the NORA-50 mast — deserves particular attention because it is the operational flagship of the entire India-Japan defence-industrial cooperation architecture. It is India-Japan's first-ever co-development and co-production project in the defence sector. It is the first Japanese defence technology transfer to India under the 2015 defence transfer agreement. And it is the substantive symbol of what the relationship is progressively becoming.

Technically, UNICORN is a composite radar mast that consolidates multiple externally-mounted antennas — communication, surveillance, electronic warfare — into a single integrated structure. This substantially reduces the warship's radar cross-section (the visibility signature that adversary sensors can pick up), substantially improves the vessel's stealth profile, and substantially strengthens the ship's overall electromagnetic survivability in contested combat environments. On Japan's Mogami-class frigates, the system has been operationally validated across multiple deployments.

The India-Japan UNICORN architecture provides for Japan to supply the core designs and engineering technology, while BEL manages system integration, production, and manufacturing in India under the Make in India framework. India also plans to integrate its own sensors and antennas into the mast for domestic Navy applications. The programme is expected to progressively replace the existing communication and sensor mast systems used across the Indian Navy's fleet.

The chronology of the UNICORN programme illustrates the substantial diplomatic and regulatory groundwork required to reach the current operational phase. August 2024 — Japanese Defence Minister Minoru Kihara offered UNICORN to India during the 2+2 dialogue in New Delhi. November 2024 — the Memorandum of Implementation was signed at the Embassy of India in Tokyo, with India's Ambassador Sibi George and ATLA Commissioner Ishikawa Takeshi exchanging the instruments. July 2026 — the 16th India-Japan Annual Summit elevated the programme to full co-development status. August 2026 — the Maritime Security Cooperation MoA fast-tracks operational implementation.

## Koizumi's visit — the operational architecture

Japanese Defence Minister Shinjiro Koizumi's two-day maiden visit to India — his first since taking office in the Sanae Takaichi Cabinet — was structured as one of the most substantive single defence-diplomacy engagements between the two countries in recent years.

Before the New Delhi meetings, Koizumi visited the Western Naval Command headquarters in Mumbai on 20 August, where he held discussions with Chief of the Naval Staff Admiral Krishna Swaminathan and the Flag Officer Commanding-in-Chief, Western Naval Command. The Mumbai leg provided operational context for the ministerial discussions that would follow — Koizumi observed the Indian Navy's forward-deployed Western Fleet infrastructure, met with commanders responsible for India's Arabian Sea operational architecture, and reviewed the specific operational environments in which the two navies will progressively cooperate.

In New Delhi, Koizumi met Prime Minister Narendra Modi at the PM's residence for what Modi described on X as "an excellent conversation on deepening defence and security ties between India and Japan". The bilateral meeting with Defence Minister Rajnath Singh followed, culminating in the Maritime Security Cooperation MoA signing and the release of the detailed joint statement outlining the broad areas of future defence and maritime cooperation.

## Why Japan is opening now — the Takaichi transformation

The substantial expansion of India-Japan defence-industrial cooperation in 2025-26 has been enabled by one of the most consequential single reforms Japan has undertaken in its post-1945 defence policy. In April 2024, Tokyo revised the "Three Principles on Transfer of Defence Equipment and Technology" — the framework that had substantially constrained Japanese defence exports since 1967 under the original Three Principles of arms export ban. The revised framework substantially eases restrictions on the sale and sharing of military technology with partner countries.

The Sanae Takaichi government, which took office in the recent Japanese electoral cycle, has substantially accelerated this trajectory. Takaichi has been publicly explicit that Japan's security environment — dominated by China's expanding military capabilities and North Korea's advancing missile programme — requires the country to build substantially deeper security partnerships across the Indo-Pacific. India, as one of the largest democracies in the region, one of the most substantial Indo-Pacific naval powers, and one of the most substantial partners for Japanese industrial cooperation, sits at the operational centre of Tokyo's expanded partnership architecture.

For India, the timing is equally consequential. India's own defence-industrial base has progressively been expanding under the Aatmanirbhar Bharat framework. The Sixth Positive Indigenisation List (documented in Bharat Samvad's 18 August Page 1 coverage) captures 405 additional items now to be sourced exclusively from Indian suppliers. The tanker vessel exports surge to $1.36 billion in Q1 FY27 (documented in the 19 August Page 2 coverage) demonstrates India's expanding shipbuilding capacity. And the India-Armenia defence R&D MoU (documented in the 20 August Page 1 coverage) demonstrates India's operational shift from arms buyer to arms co-producer. The India-Japan pact adds one of the most consequential single dimensions to this accelerating trajectory.

## Beyond bilateral — Indo-Pacific Logistics Network expansion

One of the substantial dimensions of Thursday's engagement is the expansion of the Indo-Pacific Logistics Network beyond the bilateral India-Japan architecture. Both ministers welcomed the first India-Japan-Indonesia trilateral naval PASSEX exercise, held in February 2026, and agreed to progressively deepen cooperation with third countries through high-level exchanges, exercises, and other initiatives.

This trilateral dimension is significant because it substantially extends the operational reach of the India-Japan partnership across Southeast Asia and into the wider Indo-Pacific theatre. Indonesia, positioned astride the substantial maritime chokepoints connecting the Indian Ocean and the Western Pacific, brings substantial operational geography to the trilateral architecture. Additional third-country cooperation with Philippines, Vietnam, Australia and other Indo-Pacific naval powers is progressively under discussion.

The wider Quadrilateral Security Dialogue (Quad) — India, Japan, Australia, and the United States — provides the strategic anchor for this expanding architecture, though Thursday's bilateral pact operates on its own institutional track separate from Quad's four-way framework.

## The military-to-military expansion

Alongside the maritime pact, India and Japan agreed to expand military-to-military engagement across all three services. The Dharma Guardian army exercise and JAIMEX maritime exercise have both been progressively strengthened. INS Sahyadri's participation in JAIMEX-25 (16-18 October 2025, followed by a Yokosuka port call on 21 October) demonstrated the substantially deepened operational cooperation between the two navies. The upcoming Veer Guardian 26 bilateral air exercise will be particularly significant — Japanese Air Self-Defence Force fighter aircraft will participate in India for the first time, opening the third-service leg of the trilateral service architecture.

The two countries further agreed to promote exchanges between their respective Special Operations Forces and to explore cooperation with India's integrated theatre commands after their establishment. The integrated theatre commands — one of India's most substantial single defence-reform initiatives currently under implementation — will progressively become one of the substantial institutional interfaces through which India-Japan defence cooperation operates at the operational-command level.

## What it signifies

For India's defence-industrial base, the 20 August pact provides substantial access to Japanese design expertise, engineering technology, and manufacturing know-how across categories where Japan has been one of the world's most substantial technology leaders for decades. Every jointly-developed system deployed in Indian Navy service is one substantial new capability the country has acquired without needing to build the underlying research and development infrastructure from scratch.

For Japan's defence-industrial base, the pact provides access to one of the largest single defence markets outside the traditional Western procurement architecture. Japanese firms progressively gain scale advantages through Indian production runs, third-country market opportunities through Indian export relationships (as with Armenia), and substantial operational validation of Japanese systems in Indian Navy service.

For the Indo-Pacific maritime security architecture, the substantially deeper India-Japan cooperation strengthens the operational counter-weight to Chinese naval expansion across both the Indian Ocean Region and the Western Pacific. Every MDA information exchange substantially strengthens the composite surveillance capability. Every joint exercise substantially strengthens interoperability. Every co-produced system substantially strengthens the shared industrial base that underpins sustained operational readiness.

For India-Japan bilateral relations, the pact represents the operational elevation of the Special Strategic and Global Partnership established in 2014 into its most substantive defence-industrial phase. Beyond hydrocarbons trade, Japanese investment across automobiles and semiconductors, the bullet train and metro infrastructure projects, and substantial cultural cooperation, defence-industrial partnership now progressively becomes one of the substantial pillars of the wider bilateral relationship.

For India's Aatmanirbhar Bharat framework, the pact adds another substantial dimension to the accelerating self-reliance trajectory. Alongside the Armenia R&D MoU (20 August), the tanker vessel export surge (19 August), the Sixth PIL (18 August), and the wider defence-manufacturing arc, joint R&D and co-production with Japan now progressively join the roster of institutional arrangements through which India has crossed from arms importer to arms co-producer.

For now, on the morning of 21 August 2026, what stands on the institutional record is this: the India-Japan Maritime Security Cooperation Memorandum of Arrangement has been signed; the framework for joint naval ship design and manufacturing, defence technology transfers, MDA information sharing, reciprocal ship repair, and UNICORN fast-tracking has been formally established; DRDO and Japan's ATLA will deepen R&D cooperation; a new Defence Industry Forum will be convened; the trilateral India-Japan-Indonesia PASSEX architecture will be progressively expanded; and one of the most operationally consequential single defence-diplomacy engagements of India's 2026 calendar has been successfully concluded. From maritime security agreement to joint co-production partnership — the India-Japan relationship has entered its most substantial defence-industrial phase.
