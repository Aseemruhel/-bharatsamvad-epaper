LEAD: India's supercomputing ecosystem has progressively moved from a single pioneering machine in 1991 to a nationwide architecture of 40 operational systems delivering a combined 68 petaflops of computing power. The transformation has been delivered under the National Supercomputing Mission (NSM), launched in 2015 with an outlay of approximately Rs 4,500 crore and jointly steered by the Department of Science and Technology (DST) and the Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology (MeitY). The Mission is implemented by the Centre for Development of Advanced Computing (C-DAC) in Pune and the Indian Institute of Science (IISc) in Bengaluru. Its target is 50 supercomputers delivering a combined capacity of more than 123 petaflops — and the ecosystem is now progressively on track to reach it.

## What a supercomputer actually does

A supercomputer is a computer that processes very large amounts of data at very high speeds by using many processors working in parallel on the same problem. Where a laptop might have 8 or 16 processor cores, a supercomputer uses thousands or hundreds of thousands. This is called parallel processing — many calculations happening simultaneously.

Supercomputer speed is measured in FLOPS, which stands for Floating Point Operations Per Second — the number of mathematical calculations the machine can perform in one second. One teraflop (TF) equals one trillion calculations per second. One petaflop (PF) equals one quadrillion. For context, the world's fastest supercomputer in 2026 operates at around 2.19 exaflops — 2.19 quintillion calculations per second.

## The journey — from PARAM 8000 to PARAM Rudra

India's supercomputing story began in 1991 with PARAM 8000, developed by C-DAC. Its speed was 1 gigaflop — modest by today's standards, but a substantive moment at the time. It gave India entry into the small group of countries capable of designing and building their own high-performance computers, at a point when the United States had restricted export of such technology to India.

The PARAM series progressively evolved. PARAM Yuva reached 54 teraflops and supported computationally intensive applications like weather forecasting and fluid dynamics simulation. Successive generations expanded capability further. The current flagship — the PARAM Rudra series — represents the operational outcome of NSM's self-reliance strategy. PARAM Rudra systems are built using indigenously designed Rudra servers and a fully home-grown system software stack. As of September 2026, 6,000 Rudra servers had been deployed across PARAM Rudra supercomputers, with another 1,500 servers under manufacturing.

## The 40 supercomputers now operational

The 40 systems deployed under NSM across the country break down into three operational tiers. Thirteen are high-end systems delivering more than 1 petaflop each. Twelve are mid-range systems with capacities between 500 teraflops and 1 petaflop. The remaining fifteen are smaller systems below 500 teraflops. Together they deliver the 68-petaflop combined national capacity, serving research institutions, universities, government departments and strategic agencies across the country.

## Why self-reliance in supercomputing matters

For much of the past four decades, supercomputing technology has been a tightly controlled strategic category globally. The United States and other major producers have periodically restricted the export of advanced computing systems and components to countries including India. For an aspiring technological power, dependence on imported supercomputing hardware represents a critical vulnerability — one that can be exploited at any moment of strategic tension.

NSM's architecture addresses this vulnerability directly. By progressively building the entire supercomputing stack within India — Rudra servers designed by C-DAC and manufactured by Indian electronics firms, high-speed interconnects developed and tested domestically, cooling technology engineered locally, and HPC software written in-country — India is progressively eliminating the import-dependence that previously constrained the sector. The Rudra server technology is being transferred to Indian Electronics Manufacturing Services (EMS) partners for mass production, progressively building a domestic supercomputing component industry alongside the end machines themselves.

## What the machines actually do for India

Supercomputing is not an abstract capability. The applications built on NSM infrastructure deliver direct outcomes across sectors.

In genomics and drug discovery, the platform screens candidate drugs against disease targets and predicts side effects — work that proved operationally valuable during COVID-19 when it was used to screen existing drugs for repurposing and to predict cardiac risks from potential candidates. In urban planning, the Urban Environment Decision Support System uses detailed atmospheric models to forecast heavy rainfall and air pollution, giving city authorities time to prepare mitigation measures. In energy, the Seismic Imaging Suite maps subsurface geology to improve oil and gas exploration, matching the performance of commercial international tools. In disaster management, the Flood Prediction System delivers two-day advance forecasts for the Mahanadi river basin, protecting communities and infrastructure. In environmental protection, the Forest Fire Spread Model combines satellite imagery with computational simulation to predict how wildfires will progress, tested in the Sikkim Himalayas.

These applications feed directly into the decisions made by the India Meteorological Department, the Central Water Commission, the Central Pollution Control Board and the Ministry of AYUSH, among others — turning computational capacity into operational government output.

## Building the next generation of users

Hardware is only half the story. NSM has progressively built the human-capital architecture needed to use the machines effectively. The ecosystem has supported over 16,000 researchers across 400+ institutions, including more than 2,900 PhD scholars, with over 1.5 crore compute jobs executed and 1,990 research publications contributed to as of September 2026.

To expand this user base further, the Mission runs multiple training tracks: workshops on HPC and deep learning at NSM institutions nationwide; hackathons and bootcamps providing hands-on experience in HPC, AI and generative AI; faculty development programmes in collaboration with AICTE; the annual EduHPC Workshop training faculty across the country; a structured HPC course on the SWAYAM platform under NPTEL; the HPC Shiksha portal providing recorded lectures and resources; and the NSM Users Forum where researchers across NSM sites exchange knowledge. The National Knowledge Network (NKN) connects supercomputing facilities across institutions through a high-speed national network, allowing researchers in one location to access computing resources elsewhere.

## ESTIC-2026 — the showcase moment

The next showcase for India's supercomputing achievements will be ESTIC-2026 — the second edition of India's Emerging Science, Technology and Innovation Conclave — scheduled for 27 to 29 October 2026 at Bharat Mandapam in New Delhi. Organised by the Council of Scientific and Industrial Research (CSIR) under the theme "Creative Minds, Catalyzing Science, Empowering Technologies", the three-day event will feature supercomputing alongside other emerging technology frontiers across twelve thematic areas, with plenary sessions, technical discussions and exhibitions. It is designed to catalyse strategic collaborations supporting India's Viksit Bharat 2047 vision.

## What it signals

For India's scientific research base, the 40-system / 68-petaflop supercomputing capacity progressively opens the door to research domains that were previously beyond the country's computational reach — advanced genomics, large-scale climate modelling, astrophysical simulation, materials science at atomic resolution. For India's strategic sovereignty, the home-grown Rudra servers and indigenous software stack progressively eliminate the vulnerability that imported supercomputing hardware once created. For India's digital economy, with the country now generating nearly 20 per cent of the world's data, domestic supercomputing capacity provides the computational backbone that AI, weather forecasting, space research and other data-intensive sectors increasingly require. For India's workforce, 16,000 researchers trained on NSM infrastructure progressively represent one of the largest skilled HPC user bases of any emerging economy.

From PARAM 8000 in 1991 to PARAM Rudra in 2026. From a single gigaflop to a combined 68 petaflops. From restricted imports to indigenous servers manufactured by Indian firms. From a handful of early adopters to 16,000 researchers across 400+ institutions. India's supercomputing journey captures, in one Mission, what sustained national investment in strategic technology progressively delivers over three decades.
